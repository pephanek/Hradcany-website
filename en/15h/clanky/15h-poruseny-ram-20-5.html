15h – a broken frame at 20/V

Going through material from the fifth printing plate I came across a small thing that repeats itself: on part of the stamps from field 20 the bottom-right corner of the design frame is broken. As the same object returns on the same field on nine different copies, this is not damage to an individual stamp but a constant plate flaw.

What the flaw looks like

In its undamaged state the bottom-right corner of the frame forms a clean right angle — both the outer frame line and the thin white gap that separates it from the ornamental band run around the corner without a break. On stamps with the flaw the corner turns into a continuous blot: the white gap is filled in at the corner, the lines merge, and the frame loses its sharp shape at that spot.

The position gallery shows 20/V in the state without the flaw — the flaw therefore arose in the course of printing, as at 11/II or 60/VI. The cause is unknown; at both of those fields mechanical damage to the plate during handling or cleaning suggests itself, and the same is likely here.

The survey rests on perforations A, B and C — the three perforations documented on plate V besides the imperforate form.

At first glance one is tempted to read this as: the plate was damaged only after the sheets perforated A had been dealt with. The figures in the statistics will not carry that conclusion:

  • Perforation A is scarce on plates V–VI: 501 pieces, i.e. roughly 5 % of the material of these plates, against about 47 % each for B and C. Among nine copies with the flaw the expected number of A copies would be about 0.5 — so a nil find proves nothing on its own.
  • The earliest documented usages rather argue against it: A on plates 5–6 is documented from 25 March 1920, whereas B from 8 February 1920 and C from 22 February 1920. On these plates perforation A appears last, not first.
  • Perforating was moreover the final operation and need not have followed the printing of a sheet immediately. The perforation group therefore says only approximately when a sheet was printed, and works as a dating tool only as a rough guide.

What remains is a sober statement: the flaw is documented in perforations B and C, the two groups that make up the overwhelming majority of the material of plate V. Whether it also occurs in perforation A and in the imperforate form remains open — and that is the most useful place for further finds to aim at.

The postage-due overprint

One of the documented copies carries the 30 DOPLATIT overprint. The damaged state of the plate thus also reached sheets that were later used for the postage-due overprints. For collectors this means that 20/V with the broken frame is worth looking for beyond the ordinary postage stamps.

What next

Nine copies are too few for firm conclusions. I would welcome reports of further copies of 20/V — especially in perforation A and in the imperforate form — and equally of copies without the flaw carrying a legibly dated cancellation: only cancellation dates can place the development of the flaw in time more precisely than the perforation groups can.
